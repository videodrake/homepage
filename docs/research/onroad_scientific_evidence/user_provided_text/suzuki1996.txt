Effects of Thiamine Supplementation on Exercise-induced
Fatigue
Masashige Suzukil ,3, and Yoshinori Itokawa 2
High-dose thiamine (vitamin B t ) supplementation (100 mg/day) may be helpful in
preventing or accelerating recovery from exercise-induced fatigue . Sixteen volunteer
male athletes volunteer
, 8 with a blood thiamine level of 40 ng/ml or more (normal
thiamine group) and 8 with levels below that level (low thiamine group) were selected as
subjects .
They exercised on a bicycle ergometer and the effects of thiamine
supplementation were compared with placebo . Blood thiamine level markedly increased
following supplementation of thiamine for 3 days before exercise . Exercise-induced
changes in hemodynamic parameters and cardiopulmonary function indicated the onset of
fatigue .
Thiamine supplementation significantly suppressed the increase in blood
glucose in the normal thiamine group and significantly decreased the number of
complaints shortly after exercise in the subjective fatigue assessment of 30 items .
Keywords : thiamine, fursultiamine (TTFD), athletes, exercise-induced fatigue,
blood glucose level, subjective fatigue symptoms
INTRODUCTION
Vitamin B I (thiamine) plays an important role as a coenzyme in energy metabolism and
in the maintenance of nerve cell function (Itokawa, 1975 ; Von Muralt, 1962). This
suggests that thiamine might be effective not only in the prevention of or recovery from
exercise-induced fatigue, but also in the improvement of mental and sensory fatigue, such as
languor and malaise .
In the present study, healthy adult volunteers exercised on a bicycle ergometer and the
effects of thiamine supplementation on the prevention of and recovery from exercise-induced
fatigue were evaluated using a series of physiological measures and the subjective
evaluation of fatigue .

SUBJECTS and METHODS
A medical checkup was performed on 81 volunteer male athletes aged 19 years or more,
and the blood thiamine level was determined . Sixty-one out of 81 male college-athlete
volunteers had a thiamine level lower than 40 ng/ml and the mean blood thiamine level was
38 .5±8 .8 ng/ml. Of these volunteers, 32 (40%) were shown to have a blood thiamine level
of between 41 and 68 ng/ml and 49 (60%) to have a level less than 40 ng/ml .
No
individual had a blood thiamine level below 20ng/ml . Eight individuals from both the
normal (>40 ng/ml) and low (<_40ng/ml) groups were selected as subjects (Table 1) . All of
the subjects were well informed of the nature and contents of this study by the investigator
and gave their consent to participate in this study .
Table 1 . Mean (± SD) age, body weight and height and range of blood thiamine levels for

Test drug :
The thiamine preparation contained 100 mg of thiamine as fursultiamine
(Thiamine Tetrahydrofurfuryl Disulfide, TTFD), thiamin derivative, as well as vitamins B2
(10 mg), B 6 (10 mg), and B 12 (20 µg), in 2 tablets . Placebo tablets were identical in
appearance to the active tablets and contained lactose as the major component
.
Experimental schedule: A comparative study of the thiamine preparation versus placebo
was performed in a cross-over manner (Table 2) . The subjects took 2 tablets of the vitamin
preparation or placebo with water after breakfast for 3 days prior to the day of exercise
.
They took 2 more tablets after supper on the day of exercise to facilitate the recovery from
fatigue. Half of each group took the active tablets first, and the others took placebo tablets
first. A washout period of 3-9 days was allowed between the completion of the first test
session and the start of the second supplementation .
A medical interview to confirm general health and to draw blood samples was performed
before thiamine supplementation and bicycle ergometer exercise . Meals and physical
activity during the supplementation period were recorded .

Assessment of thiamine effect :
The subjects exercised on a bicycle ergometer for 30
min (60% of VO 2 Max .) at 1 h after breakfast of the day of testing. Evaluation items and
hemodynamic parameters were measured before, immediately after (0 min), and at 30, 60,
and 120 min after the exercise . In addition, subjective symptoms were recorded the next
morning .
Subjective fatigue was evaluated as generalized fatigue on a scale of 5 and using a
questionnaire of 30 items prepared by the Industrial Fatigue Study Group of the Japanese
Society for Industrial Hygiene (Sangyo Hiro Kenkyukai) of Japan Society for Occupational
Health (Table 2) . In the latter evaluation, the subjects marked the items they felt, from the
list of 30 items . A larger number denotes a more fatigued condition .
Statistical analysis : Category variables were analyzed by the Mann-Whitney U-test, and
continuous variables by the t-test for cross-over design
.

Subjective Findings
Subjective symptom evaluation using 30 items indicated significant improvement
immediately after exercise when thiamine supplementation was given (p<0 .05) (Figure 3) .
Statistical analyses revealed separately, no significant differences were noted in either
normal thiamine group and low thiamine group, but subjective fatigue was significantly
alleviated in both groups with thiamine supplementation (p<0 .01) .
In the ten items (A) in Table 2, which indicate general languishness, the effect of
thiamine supplementation was significantly better in the low thiamine group and in the

DISCUSSION
Many aspects of fatigue have been studied over the years, but adequate parameters for
objective evaluation of fatigue have not yet been established . In the present study, healthy
adult male athletes exercised to fatigue, and the effect of thiamine supplementation on
fatigue was then evaluated .
The results of a medical checkup performed before selection of subjects revealed that
61% (49/81) of those surveyed had a blood thiamine level less than the lower limit of
normal (40 ng/ml), indicating a marginal or latent deficit. It is unknown whether or not the
levels among athletes are different from those found in the general population . It is reported
that oarsmen who consumed energy of 4000 kcal or more a day took a larger amount of
thiamine than the recommended requirement, but 11 of 14 oarsmen were judged to have a
marginal deficiency (Kobayashi, 1990) . This suggests that the thiamine requirement of
athletes may be larger than that recommended for non-athletes
.
Fursultiamine ('1 !FD), a thiamine derivative, has been proven to be absorbed efficiently
from the intestine, to have a large tissue distribution, and to be readily converted into
thiamine . Therefore, TTFD is expected to have a greater physiological effect than
conventional thiamine because of improved absorption (Itokawa et al., 1992). TTFD has
been widely used not only in the treatment of thiamine deficiency and various diseases with
possible energy metabolism disorders but also to prevent or accelerate the recovery from
exercise-induced fatigue . Studies have shown that TTFD, which was supplemented to
physical workers in a high-temperature environment (Nakamura, 1971) and to athletes in a
training camp for 10 days to 2 weeks (Nishiyama et al., 1972), was effective in the recovery
from chronic physical fatigue . When conventional thiamine preparations were
supplemented (10 mg/day) to elderly people with marginal thiamine deficiency for 6 weeks,
various benefits such as increased appetite, improved general well-being, and decreased
fatigue were observed (Smidt, 1991)
.
As shown in these reports, long-term
supplementation of thiamine preparations has been demonstrated to be effective in
accelerating the recovery from fatigue, but the effects of short-term thiamine
supplementation (3 days) on recovery from acute fatigue due to single exercise have not yet
been studied . Although the effects on recovery from fatigue should be evaluated on
substances administered after the onset of fatigue, it was suggested in one report that
thiamine must be stored in the muscles before exercise (Tsuji, 1991) . Accordingly, in the
present study, TTFD was supplemented for 3 days before exercise to evaluate its effect .

TTFD was also supplemented in the evening on the day of exercise and its effect on the
recovery from fatigue was evaluated the next morning .
In the present study, the number of complaints regarding subjective fatigue were
significantly decreased immediately after exercise by thiamine supplementation . In the 10
items relating to general languishness in Table 3, thiamine supplementation significantly
alleviated malaise in the total of 2 groups and the low thiamine group . This finding
substantiated our hypothesis that there might be a correlation between blood thiamine levels
and fatigue, and it may support the needs of thiamine supplementation to people suspected
of having insufficient thiamine levels, e .g. those who are unbalanced diet eaters, liable to
fatigue, heavy alcohol drinkers, hard workers, competitive athletes, etc . In the 10 items
relating to spiritual concentration, thiamine supplementation significantly suppressed the
exercise-induced lowering of spiritual concentration in the normal thiamine group . The
reason for this is unknown, though we might speculate that thiamine played a role other
than just a supplementary one . But all in all, there was no clear difference in the effect of
thiamine between the low thiamine and normal thiamine groups . This may be due to the
fact that the thiamine in the low thiamine group is only marginally deficient, and that there
was no marked difference in the blood thiamine level between the low thiamine (mean 30.9
± 7.7 ng/ml) and normal thiamine (mean 56.8 ± 15.0 ng/ml) groups . A larger number of
volunteers may be needed to study this subject further . Thiamine supplementation had no
effect on subjective fatigue symptoms measured the morning after the exercise, but this
may have been because the subjects were young athletes who could quickly recover from
exercise-induced fatigue .
Thiamine supplementation influenced neither the increase in the plasma lactate level nor
the decrease in the serum free fatty acid level caused by muscular exercise . Respiratory
quotient, which is defined as the ratio of exhaled carbon dioxide gas to inhaled oxygen gas,
was not affected by exercise . This phenomenon might be explained by the fact that the
subjects were athletes and well trained . Thiamine supplementation did suppress the increase
in the blood glucose level in the normal thiamine group . This finding is difficult to
interpret, but thiamine, as a coenzyme, may enhance the energy metabolism of glucose in
some way (Takahashi, 1981) . Namely, ingestion of a sufficient amount of thiamine might
increase pyruvate dehydrogenase activity in the glycolytic pathway and a-ketoglutarate
dehydrogenase activity in the TCA cycle . Hence, thiamine may promote energy production
by utilizing glucose efficiently as an energy source . It is unknown whether this possible
effect on glucose is related to the suppressive effect of thiamine supplementation on the
lowering of spiritual concentration seen in the normal thiamine group .
CONCLUSION
Sixteen young male athletes were divided into a low thiamine group (<40 ng/ml) and a
normal thiamine group (>_40 ng/ml), and the effects of thiamine supplementation on
exercise-induced fatigue were compared with placebo .
