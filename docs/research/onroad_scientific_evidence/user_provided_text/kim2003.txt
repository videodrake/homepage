Octacosanol Supplementation Increases Running Endurance Time and Improves
Biochemical Parameters After Exhaustion in Trained Rats
Hyunjung Kim,1 Sunghee Park,1 Dae Suk Han,2 and Taesun Park1
1Department of Food and Nutrition, Yonsei University, Seoul; and 2Korea Food Research Institute, 
Kyunggi-do, Korea
ABSTRACT
This study evaluated the effects of octacosanol on running performance and related biochemical parameters in
exercise-trained rats run to exhaustion on a treadmill. Male Sprague–Dawley rats were randomly assigned to one of three
groups—sedentary control group (SC), exercise-trained control group (EC), and exercise-trained, octacosanol-supplemented
group (EO)—and raised on either control or octacosanol (0.75%)-supplemented diet with (or without for SC rats) exercise-
training for 4 weeks. EC rats ran 184% longer until exhaustion than SC rats (P , .01), while octacosanol-supplemented trained
rats ran 46% longer than EC rats (P , .05). Under the exhausted state immediately following the running performance test,
EO rats exhibited significantly higher plasma ammonia and lactate concentrations compared with the values for EC rats (P ,
.05). Although EO rats ran significantly longer until exhausted, their plasma glucose level and gastronecmius muscle glyco-
gen concentration were not significantly different from those of EC rats. Dietary supplementation of octacosanol resulted in
significantly higher creatine phosphokinase activity in plasma (44% increase, P , .01) and citrate synthase activity in mus-
cle (16% increase, P , .01) of exercise-trained rats. These results suggest that the ergogenic properties of octacosanol include
the sparing of muscle glycogen stores and increases in the oxidative capacity in the muscle of exercise-trained rats.

INTRODUCTION
O
VER THE PAST 10 YEARS, nutritional supplements have
become increasingly popular among the general public
worldwide, and many supplements are taken as ergogenic
aids, a term used for substances that enhance athletic per-
formance and increase stamina and exercise capacity. Er-
gogenic aids are believed to increase athletic performance
by either renewing or increasing energy stores in the body,
modulating the biochemical reactions contributing to fa-
tigue, or maintaining optimal body weight.1
Octacosanol [HO-CH2-(CH2)26CH3], a high-molecular-
weight, primary aliphatic alcohol, is the main component of
a natural wax product that naturally occurs in fruits and
leaves and on the surface of plants and whole seeds. Most
studies evaluating the efficacy of octacosanol have used ei-
ther a wheat-germ oil extract (policosanol) or a natural mix-
ture of primary alcohols isolated from sugar cane wax (Sac-
charum officinarum L.), of which octacosanol is the main
component.2,3 Recent studies in humans and rabbits with hy-
percholesterolemia4–9 have demonstrated that octacosanol is
involved in the modulation of cholesterol metabolism, low-
ering blood low-density lipoprotein-cholesterol and elevat-
ing high-density lipoprotein-cholesterol concentrations. In
addition to the cholesterol-lowering effects, anti-aggrega-
tory properties and the cytoprotective activity of octacosanol
have been investigated.
Ergogenic properties of octacosanol were first postulated
by Cureton10 from the observation that extracts from wheat-
germ oil (octacosanol) had beneficial effects on the physi-
cal performance of athletes. Another early study on octa-
cosanol involved the investigation of its effects on metabolic
responses to submaximal cycle ergometry, grip and chest
strength, and reaction time in human subjects.2 More re-
cently, studies11,12 have suggested beneficial effects of oc-
tacosanol on physical performance in different systems.
Most of these studies assessing the use of octacosanol on
athletic performance have been conducted in humans using
experimental designs lacking randomization, and appear to
be nonspecific in terms of sports regimens and diet. Also,
many claims for improving athletic performance as well as
its underlying mechanism have yet to be proven in an ap-
propriate and controlled animal model. The present study
was, therefore, undertaken to evaluate effects of octacosanol,
used as a dietary supplement, on endurance exercise per-
formance in exercise-trained rats. Several biochemical pa-
rameters, such as blood concentrations of lactate, ammonia,
glucose, and inorganic phosphorus, along with muscle
glycogen concentration and enzyme activities involved in
the energy production pathway during exercise have been
assessed.

MATERIALS AND METHODS
Animals and experimental diets
Twenty-four male Sprague–Dawley rats weighing be-
tween 180 and 200 g were purchased from Daehan Labora-
tory Animal Research Center Co. (Chungcheongbuk-do,
Korea). Rats were acclimated to individual stainless steel
cages in a room with controlled temperature (20–23°C), hu-
midity (50–60%), and lighting (12-hour light–dark cycle)
and fed a commercial pelleted rat chow for 5 days prior to
initiating the experimental diets. The rats were randomly as-
signed to one of the following experimental groups: seden-
tary control group (SC; n 5 8), exercise-trained control
group (EC; n 5 8), and exercise-trained, octacosanol-sup-
plemented group (EO; n 5 8).
Rats in the SC and EC groups were fed the control diet
formulated using the AIN-93G purified rodent diet, and an-
imals in the EO group were fed the same diet supplemented
with 0.75% octacosanol for 4 weeks. Compositions of the
experimental diets are as listed in Table 1. Animals were
given free access to food and drinking water for the entire
feeding period. Food consumption was recorded daily, and
body weight gain was measured every third day between
1200 and 1300 h. This study adhered to the Guide for the
Care and Use of Laboratory Animals developed by the In-
stitute of Laboratory Animal Resources of the National Re-
search Council,13 and was approved by the Institutional An-
imal Care and Use Committee of Yonsei University, Seoul,
South Korea.
Exercise training protocol and running 
performance test
Rats in EC and EO groups were exercise-trained for the
entire experimental period on a treadmill, at room temper-
ature, 5 days/week. After being familiarized with a motor-
driven rodent treadmill (Daejong Instrument Co., Seoul) for
2 days, animals were forced to run on a treadmill at 15
m/minute, 0% slope for 20 minutes/day during the first
week. The intensity and duration of the exercise training
were progressively increased until at week 4 the animals
were running at 25 m/minute, 0% slope for 60 minutes/day
as shown in Table 2.
At the end of the experimental period, animals in the SC,
EC, and EO groups were all food-deprived for 2 hours, and
then tested for their endurance exercise performance on a
treadmill. The speed began with 12 m/minute, and was in-
creased every 3 minutes by 3 m/minute until reaching 30
m/minute. Rats were run until they could no longer main-
tain the pace of the treadmill. Once a rat could not keep pace
with the treadmill for up to 10 seconds, it was removed from
the treadmill and placed in a supine position. If the rat did
not right itself, it was considered exhausted and immediately

Sample collection
Immediately after the end of the running performance test,
exhausted animals were anesthetized with ethyl ether, and
the abdomen was opened. Blood samples were collected
from the abdominal vein into a heparinized syringe, and
plasma was prepared by a low-speed centrifugation (2,000
g for 10 minutes). Gastrocnemius and soleus muscles were
surgically removed from both left and right hind limbs,
weighed after trimming away connective tissues, freeze-
clamped in liquid nitrogen, and stored at 280°C until analy-
ses.
Analytical methods
Activity of plasma creatine phosphokinase (CK) (Bio
Clinical System Corp., Anyang, Kyunggi-do, South Korea)
and plasma concentrations of ammonia (Wako Pure Chem-
ical, Osaka, Japan), lactate (Sigma, St. Louis, MO), glucose
(Bio Clinical System Corp.), and inorganic phosphorus (Bio
Clinical System Corp.) were analyzed using commercially
available kits.
Citrate synthase (CS) activity was measured in soleus
muscles as an index of the training-induced increase in ox-
idative capacity. Samples of thawed muscle tissue were
minced and homogenized in ice-cold 100 mM potassium
phosphate buffer with 0.05% bovine serum albumin (1:20
wt/vol, pH 7.4). Homogenates were then centrifuged at 400
g for 10 minutes. The supernatant was decanted and assayed
for CS activity at 37°C using the procedure described by
Srere.14 Activity of lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) was de-
termined according to the method of Pesce et al.15 Gas-
trocnemius muscles were homogenized in ice-cold 100 mM
potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) using a Polytron ho-
mogenizer. The assay was performed in a reaction mixture
containing 100 mM KH2PO4 (pH 7.5), 3.6 mM nicotinamide
adenine dinucleotide, and 3.3 mM pyruvate. Protein con-
centration was determined using reagents from Bio-Rad
Laboratories (Hercules, CA). Enzyme activities were ex-
pressed in mmol (or nmol) mg of wet muscle21 min21.
Glycogen content of the gastrocnemius muscle was de-
termined by the method of Chun and Yin16 with minor mod-
ifications. The gastrocnemius muscle (0.2 g) was added to
0.5 mL of 30% KOH, and the vials were kept at 100°C for
20 minutes. After addition of 1.5 mL of anhydrous ethanol,
the vials were centrifuged at 4,000 g for 15 minutes, and the
supernatants were discarded. Distilled water (0.5 mL) and
1 mL of 0.2% anthrone (0.2 g of anthrone in 100 mL of
freshly prepared 98% H2SO4) were added, and the vials were
placed in boiling water for 20 minutes. The optical density
of the reaction mixture was determined by photometry at
620 nm.

Statistical analyses
Data are expressed as mean 6 SEM values of eight rats.
All statistical analyses were performed using SAS/STAT
version 8.1 (SAS Institute, Inc., Cary, NC). Significance of
the effect of exercise training or dietary supplementation was
analyzed by the independent samples t test at P , .05 or
P , .01. Correlations between running time as an indicator
of endurance exercise performance and plasma concentra-
tion of glucose, ammonia, lactate, or inorganic phosphorus,
as well as muscle glycogen level, of rats were analyzed by
the Pearson correlation test at P , .05.
RESULTS
The food efficiency ratio and skeletal muscle weights of
rats are presented in Table 3. Exercise training on a tread-
mill for 4 weeks with or without dietary supplementation re-
sulted in slightly lower values for cumulative body weight
gain and food efficiency ratio compared with the SC rats,
but these differences were not statistically significant (P .
.05). Rats exercise-trained without dietary supplementation
had a significantly higher gastrocnemius muscle mass (P ,
.05) with no difference in the soleus muscle weight com-
pared with those for the SC animals (P . .05). Supple-
menting trained rats with octacosanol did not affect body
weight gain, food efficiency ratio, or skeletal muscle weights
compared with the EC animals.
In this study, both exercise training and octacosanol sup-
plementation significantly affected running time to exhaus-
tion (Fig. 1). In the SC rats, mean running time to exhaus-
tion was 33.3 6 3.8 minutes (range 26–59 minutes), whereas
trained rats ran 184% longer, 94.5 6 6.6 minutes (range
76–125 minutes), which was statistically significant (P ,
.01). The EO rats ran significantly (P , .05) longer (46%)
than the trained rats without supplementation, with an av-
erage running time of 137.6 6 17.5 minutes (range 90–248
minutes).
Correlations between running time, as an indicator of en-
durance exercise performance, and the plasma concentra-
tions of diverse fatigue-related parameters as well as mus-
cle glycogen concentration were analyzed in the SC, EC,
and EO rats (Table 4). The positive correlation observed be-
tween plasma lactate concentration and running time to ex-
haustion was significant in the SC rats (P , .05), but was
without statistical significance in the exercise-trained ani-
mals with or without dietary supplementation (EO and EC,
P . .05). Running time to exhaustion was positively corre-
lated, but not significantly (P . .05), with plasma concen-
trations of ammonia and inorganic phosphorus, and nega-
tively correlated with plasma glucose concentrations (also
not significant). There was an inverse correlation of running
time with muscle glycogen concentration in all three groups,
but it was only significant in the EC rats.

DISCUSSION
Peripheral factors that contribute to exercise-induced fa-
tigue such as glycogen depletion and lactate accumulation
have been extensively studied and are well described.17 Lac-
tate is produced during glycolysis, and under normal cir-
cumstances it is further degraded via the tricarboxylic acid
cycle for the production of ATP by oxidative phosphoryla-
tion. There is also growing evidence of a significant central
(i.e., a central nervous system) component, such as plasma
ammonia level, to exercise-induced fatigue.18,19 The imme-
diate source of ammonia from muscle appears to be a result
of the deamination of AMP and is more apparent in fast-
twich than in slow-twitch fibers. In high-intensity exercise,
the increase in ADP concentration activates the enzyme
AMP deaminase, a key enzyme in the purine nucleotide cy-

The effect of the training protocol used in this study is
confirmed by the increased time to reach exhaustion ob-
served for the trained rats without dietary supplementation.
The training protocol in our study also increased the gas-
trocnemius muscle (fast-twitch fiber) weight and CS activ-
ity in the soleus muscle (slow-twitch fiber) of rats, both of
which are known to be elevated by aerobic exercise train-
ing.23–25 In this study, the exercise-trained rats that were not
supplemented appeared to accumulate ammonia and lactate
in their blood at a slower rate than did SC animals, so they
could run longer until exhausted. These data are in agree-
ment with human studies showing that trained subjects gen-
erally experience a lesser increase in plasma ammonia dur-
ing either prolonged or intense exercise.26 The major
metabolic consequences of adaptation to endurance exercise
training are a slower utilization of muscle glycogen and
blood glucose, a greater reliance on fat oxidation, and less
lactate production in the muscle during exercise of a given
intensity.27 The presence in the blood of enzymes that are
normally localized in muscle fibers is taken as evidence of
disruption or increased permeability of the muscle cell mem-
branes. In this respect, increased plasma CK is considered
one of the best indicators of muscle damage from eccentric
contraction.28 Exercise training is known to prevent the rise
of plasma CK during a single bout of exercise under a given
protocol.29,30 The reason our trained rats exhibited higher
plasma CK activity appears to be attributable to the in-
creased running time to exhaustion compared with the SC
rats.
We investigated the effect of octacosanol supplemen-
tation on the time to reach exhaustion of exercise-trained
rats subjected to a single bout of high-intensity exercise.
We chose a 0.75% octacosanol supplementation level
based on earlier reports by other investigators3,31 that oc-
tacosanol (or polycosanol) was effective in preventing my-
ocardial necrosis and modulating lipid metabolism in rats
at 0.1–1% levels. Octacosanol supplementation produced
an increase in the time to exhaustion of exercise-trained
rats. Elevations in plasma lactate and ammonia concen-
trations and CK activity following the running perfor-
mance test appear to be related to the increase in en-
durance. Although EO rats ran significantly longer until
exhausted, their plasma glucose and gastronecmius mus-
cle glycogen concentrations under the exhausted state
were not significantly different from those for EC rats.
Octacosanol supplementation significantly elevated CK
and CS activities in the muscle of exercise-trained rats.
These results can be summarized as follows: Octacosanol
supplementation per se increased the endurance exercise
performance of trained rats without down-regulating the
production of lactate and ammonia. Therefore, octa-
cosanol exerts its ergogenic activity through other bio-
chemical mechanisms involving increased activity of CS
and enhanced glycogen storage (and/or saving of glyco-
gen utilization) in trained muscle.
